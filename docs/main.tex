\section{Introduction}

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a powerful approach for 
solving sequential decision-making problems by combining reinforcement 
learning with deep neural networks. Its ability to learn complex policies 
directly from high-dimensional observations has led to applications across
various domains, including robotics, control systems, games, and financial trading.

This work presents the development of a DRL framework for multi-asset trading in stock 
and cryptocurrency markets. The framework supports the complete DRL pipeline, including 
environment modeling, data preprocessing and normalization, policy network design, training 
using algorithms such as PPO and A2C, and evaluation through backtesting on unseen data.

The objective of this project is to provide a modular framework for studying and evaluating 
DRL methods in financial environments while enabling experimentation with different 
architectures, policies, and training configurations.

The implementation of this work is based on the 
\href{https://github.com/AI4Finance-Foundation/FinRL}{FinRL} framework.


\section{Trading Environment Modeling}

The proposed DRL framework is built upon custom \texttt{Gymnasium} environments 
that formulate the trading problem as a sequential decision-making task. 
At each timestep, the agent observes the current market state and portfolio 
information, and outputs continuous actions that determine asset allocation 
decisions. Two environments are implemented: a cryptocurrency trading environment
and a stock trading environment.

\subsection{Crypto Trading Environment}

The crypto environment models a multi-asset portfolio where the agent interacts 
with price and technical indicator data over time. The environment state consists 
of both market information and portfolio status, including cash and held positions.

The action space is continuous and bounded in $[-1, 1]^N$, where each component 
represents a relative trading signal for a given asset. In practice, negative values 
correspond to sell decisions while positive values correspond to buy decisions. These 
signals are mapped to actual trading quantities using the current portfolio state. 
For selling, the agent liquidates a fraction of the currently held shares proportional 
to the magnitude of the action. For buying, the action determines a desired allocation 
scaled by the current portfolio value, which is then converted into a number of shares 
subject to available cash, transaction costs, and position limits.

The observation space is structured into two components: a portfolio vector and a market
feature vector. The portfolio vector contains the available cash and the number of held 
shares for each asset. The market vector encodes the current prices together with technical
indicators for each ticker, providing the agent with both raw and engineered market information.

An alternative formulation of the action space is based on direct portfolio allocation,
where actions are bounded in $[0, 1]^N$ and each value represents the fraction of total 
portfolio value assigned to each asset. However, this approach was not adopted because 
it requires solving a normalization constraint at every step to ensure that allocations 
sum to one. This introduces coupling between assets and makes exploration significantly 
more difficult for the agent, especially in high-dimensional multi-asset settings. 
In contrast, the chosen formulation allows independent per-asset decisions and leads to 
more stable learning dynamics.

The reward function is defined as the logarithmic change in portfolio value between 
consecutive timesteps:

\[
r_t = \log\left(\frac{V_{t+1} + \epsilon}{V_t + \epsilon}\right)
\]

The logarithmic formulation is used instead of simple returns because it provides 
numerical stability, is symmetric for gains and losses, and naturally corresponds 
to maximizing geometric growth of the portfolio over time. This makes it particularly 
suitable for long-horizon trading tasks, where compounding effects are essential.

Transaction costs and position constraints are incorporated into the environment 
dynamics to ensure realistic trading behavior. The agent is therefore required 
to learn a policy that balances profitability and trading frictions under continuous 
control constraints.

\subsection{Stock Trading Environment}

The stock trading environment follows the same general DRL formulation as the crypto
environment, modeling trading as a sequential decision-making problem under uncertainty.
However, it introduces additional structure to account for market risk signals and more 
constrained trading dynamics.

In addition to prices and technical indicators, the state representation may include 
external market descriptors such as volatility indices (e.g., VIX) and turbulence 
measures. These signals provide the agent with additional information about market 
uncertainty, allowing it to adapt its policy under different regimes of risk.

The action space remains continuous and bounded in $[-1, 1]^N$, where each component
represents a per-asset trading signal. Similarly to the crypto environment, negative
values indicate selling pressure while positive values indicate buying pressure. 
Actions are translated into discrete share operations by first applying sell 
decisions to reduce existing holdings and then executing buy decisions constrained
by available cash, transaction costs, and per-asset position limits. In addition, 
the environment enforces a maximum holding constraint per asset, which prevents 
unlimited accumulation of positions.

An additional mechanism is introduced to handle high-risk market regimes. When a 
predefined turbulence threshold is exceeded, the action is clipped such that the 
agent is biased towards reducing exposure. This effectively forces a conservative
behavior during periods of extreme market instability and encourages risk-aware 
policy learning.

The reward function extends the standard log-return formulation by incorporating a 
risk-sensitive penalty term:

\[
r_t = \log\left(\frac{V_{t+1} + \epsilon}{V_t + \epsilon}\right) - \lambda 
\cdot \text{exposure}_t \cdot \text{risk}_t
\]

where $\text{exposure}_t$ represents the fraction of capital invested in risky 
assets and $\text{risk}_t$ is derived from market volatility indicators. The 
coefficient $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between return maximization and 
risk minimization.

Overall, the stock trading environment defines a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
in which the agent observes a structured state $s_t$ composed of portfolio and
market information, takes continuous actions $a_t$, and receives a reward $r_t$
that reflects both profitability and risk exposure. The objective is to learn a
policy $\pi(a_t | s_t)$ that maximizes the expected discounted return under 
realistic market constraints and non-stationary dynamics.

\section{Policy Network Architectures}

To address the sequential and highly non-stationary nature of financial time 
series, this work investigates different policy architectures within the DRL 
framework. In particular, we compare standard MLP-based policies, recurrent 
policies (LSTM-based), and two custom Transformer-based feature extractors 
designed to capture temporal dependencies in market dynamics.

Traditional MLP policies used in DRL frameworks such as \texttt{Stable-Baselines3} 
treat observations as independent feature vectors, ignoring temporal structure.
While effective in simple environments, this assumption is limiting in financial 
trading, where decisions depend strongly on historical context.

Recurrent policies such as LSTM-based architectures partially address this limitation 
by maintaining an internal hidden state. However, they compress historical information 
into a fixed-size memory vector, which can lead to information loss over long sequences
and may struggle with long-range dependencies.

Transformers are introduced to overcome these limitations by using self-attention, 
which allows the model to directly attend to all past observations within a fixed 
lookback window. This enables more effective modeling of long-range temporal 
dependencies, regime shifts, delayed market reactions, and interactions across 
features and time steps. As a result, Transformers are particularly well-suited 
for multi-asset trading, where both temporal dynamics and cross-asset relationships 
evolve continuously over time.

\subsection{Transformer Feature Extractor (Full Fusion Model)}

The first proposed architecture is a fully Transformer-based feature extractor in
which portfolio state and market observations are jointly processed. At each timestep,
the input consists of a sequence over a fixed lookback window, where each element 
concatenates portfolio information (cash and asset holdings) with market features 
(prices and technical indicators). This vector is projected into a latent space of 
dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 128$ using a linear embedding layer.

A learnable positional embedding of shape $(1, T, 128)$ is added to preserve temporal
order. The resulting sequence is processed by a Transformer encoder composed of $2$ 
layers, each with $4$ attention heads and a feedforward network of dimension $256$,
using pre-layer normalization and dropout of $0.1$ for regularization.

Formally, the embedding step can be written as:
\[
x_t = W_{\text{proj}} \cdot [\text{portfolio}_t, \text{market}_t]
\]

The final representation is obtained by selecting the last time-step output of the 
Transformer encoder and applying layer normalization before passing it to the 
actor-critic heads of the DRL algorithm.

This architecture enables the agent to learn a unified representation that captures
both temporal dependencies and cross-asset interactions within a single attention-based 
framework.

\subsection{Hybrid MLP--Transformer Architecture}

The second proposed architecture introduces a structured separation between temporal 
market modeling and static portfolio representation. Unlike the fully Transformer-based 
model, this design explicitly decomposes the observation space into two independent 
branches before merging them into a shared latent representation.

The \textbf{market branch} processes sequential observations over a fixed lookback window. 
Each timestep contains price information and technical indicators for all assets, forming 
an input tensor of shape $(T, N \cdot (1 + F))$. This input is first projected into a latent
space of dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 128$ using a linear embedding layer. A learnable 
positional encoding of shape $(1, T, 128)$ is added, after which the sequence is processed 
by a Transformer encoder composed of $2$ layers, each with $4$ attention heads, a feedforward 
dimension of $256$, pre-layer normalization, and dropout of $0.1$. The output corresponding to 
the final timestep is then normalized using Layer Normalization to obtain the market embedding.

In parallel, the \textbf{portfolio branch} processes the most recent portfolio state, 
consisting of cash and asset holdings. Since this component is non-sequential, it is 
handled using a multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers of size $64$, each followed 
by ReLU activations. This branch produces a compact embedding that captures the current 
allocation structure of the agent.

The two representations are then fused by concatenation:
\[
z_t = [z^{\text{market}}_t, z^{\text{portfolio}}_t]
\]
followed by a fully connected layer that maps the concatenated vector back to a 
$d_{\text{model}} = 128$ dimensional space with a ReLU activation. This fusion step 
allows the policy to jointly reason over temporal market dynamics and the current 
portfolio configuration.

This architectural choice is motivated by the different nature of the two information 
sources. Market data is inherently sequential and benefits from attention-based modeling, 
whereas portfolio state is instantaneous and better suited to a feedforward representation.
Separating these streams reduces unnecessary coupling and improves interpretability while
maintaining expressive power through the fusion layer.

\section{Data Representation, Sequence Construction, and Normalization}

Since the proposed transformer architectures rely on temporal models , the environment 
observations are explicitly structured as fixed-length sequences. This is achieved through
a vectorized environment wrapper that maintains a sliding window of the last $T$ observations
for each parallel environment instance. As a result, instead of receiving a single state 
$s_t$, the agent observes a sequence $(s_{t-T+1}, \dots, s_t)$, where each state corresponds
only to information available at a single timestep $t$ (portfolio state and market features 
at that time).

This sequence construction is implemented via a \texttt{VecSequenceWrapper}, which maintains 
a rolling buffer of size $T = 10$ (as defined in the training configuration). At each environment 
step, the buffer is shifted and the newest observation is appended, ensuring that the agent always 
receives a temporally ordered window of past observations. When an episode terminates, the 
corresponding buffer is reset to avoid information leakage across episodes.

Given the strong heterogeneity in scale across financial features (e.g., prices, technical 
indicators, portfolio values, and reward magnitudes), and the non-stationary nature of 
financial time series, a normalization strategy is required. For this purpose, a rolling 
window normalization wrapper is applied both to observations and (optionally) rewards.

The \texttt{RollingWindowNorm} wrapper maintains a fixed-size buffer over the most recent 
observations and rewards and computes online estimates of mean and variance. Each input is
normalized as:

\[
\hat{x}_t = \frac{x_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t + \epsilon}
\]

where $\mu_t$ and $\sigma_t$ are computed over a sliding window of past values. This 
approach ensures that normalization adapts over time, which is crucial in non-stationary 
financial environments where data distributions shift across regimes.

In practice, this normalization improves training stability by preventing dominance of 
high-magnitude features and aligning the scale of portfolio, price, and indicator inputs.
It also helps mitigate the distribution shift between training and testing periods, which 
is particularly important for backtesting on unseen data.

\section{Reinforcement Learning Algorithms}

This work evaluates five standard Deep Reinforcement Learning algorithms, covering both 
on-policy and off-policy families. The goal is to compare how different learning paradigms 
behave under the same trading environment and feature representations.

\subsection{On-Policy Methods}

On-policy methods learn directly from data generated by the current policy, meaning that 
the policy is continuously updated using fresh trajectories collected from interaction with 
the environment. This typically leads to more stable learning dynamics, since the data 
distribution closely matches the policy being optimized. However, this also reduces sample 
efficiency, as past experiences cannot be reused.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C)} is a synchronous actor-critic algorithm that 
jointly learns a policy function (actor) and a value function (critic). The critic estimates
the expected return, while the actor updates the policy in the direction suggested by the 
advantage signal. This reduces variance compared to pure policy gradient methods and leads
to more stable updates. A2C performs updates based on multiple parallel environment steps, 
aggregating experience before performing gradient updates.

\item \textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)} improves upon standard policy gradient 
methods by constraining policy updates to avoid excessively large deviations between 
successive policies. Instead of directly maximizing expected returns, PPO optimizes a 
surrogate objective that penalizes large changes in the policy distribution. This results in a
more robust and stable training process, particularly in environments with noisy or 
non-stationary rewards such as financial markets.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Off-Policy Methods}

Off-policy methods learn from experiences stored in a replay buffer, allowing the agent 
to reuse past transitions that were generated by previous versions of the policy or even 
different exploration strategies. This significantly improves sample efficiency and enables
more stable learning in environments where data collection is expensive or limited. However, 
off-policy learning can be more sensitive to hyperparameter choices and function approximation 
errors.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)} is an off-policy actor-critic 
algorithm designed for continuous action spaces. It learns a deterministic policy and uses 
a separate critic to estimate action-value functions. The use of a replay buffer allows the 
algorithm to learn from past experiences, while exploration is typically introduced through 
noise added to the actions during training. DDPG can be unstable in practice due to 
overestimation bias and sensitivity to hyperparameters.

\item \textbf{Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3)} addresses the instability of DDPG by introducing 
several key improvements. It uses two independent critic networks to reduce overestimation 
bias, delayed policy updates to stabilize learning, and smoothing of target actions to improve
robustness. These modifications make TD3 significantly more stable and reliable in continuous 
control tasks.

\item \textbf{Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)} is an off-policy method that formulates reinforcement 
learning as an entropy-regularized optimization problem. In addition to maximizing expected 
return, SAC explicitly encourages exploration by maximizing policy entropy. This results in a
stochastic policy that remains exploratory throughout training, making SAC particularly robust
in complex and noisy environments such as financial markets.

\end{itemize}

On-policy methods such as A2C and PPO prioritize stability by learning from fresh data 
but require more environment interactions. In contrast, off-policy methods such as DDPG, 
TD3, and SAC improve sample efficiency by reusing past experiences stored in a replay 
buffer, at the cost of increased sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning and potential instability.

In all methods, the learning rate controls the magnitude of parameter updates, while 
batch-related parameters determine the statistical quality of gradient estimates. 
Exploration is explicitly regulated either through entropy regularization (on-policy
methods and SAC) or implicitly through noise in deterministic policy methods (DDPG 
and TD3). This combination of algorithms allows for a comprehensive evaluation of
different DRL learning paradigms in financial trading environments.
